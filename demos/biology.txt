The cell is the basic unit of life, and every living organism is built from one or more cells. Prokaryotic cells, such as bacteria, lack a nucleus and keep their DNA in a region called the nucleoid. Eukaryotic cells store their genetic material inside a membrane-bound nucleus. The plasma membrane is a phospholipid bilayer that controls what enters and leaves the cell. Mitochondria generate most of the cell's ATP through cellular respiration and are often called the powerhouse of the cell. Chloroplasts in plant cells capture light energy and convert it into chemical energy. Ribosomes read messenger RNA and assemble proteins from amino acids. The endoplasmic reticulum folds proteins and synthesizes lipids, while the Golgi apparatus packages and ships them. Lysosomes contain digestive enzymes that break down worn-out organelles and foreign material. The cytoskeleton, made of actin filaments and microtubules, gives the cell its shape and moves cargo along internal tracks.

DNA is a double helix made of two strands of nucleotides held together by complementary base pairs. Adenine pairs with thymine and guanine pairs with cytosine. A gene is a stretch of DNA that carries the instructions for building a specific protein. During transcription, RNA polymerase copies a gene into a molecule of messenger RNA. During translation, ribosomes read the RNA three bases at a time, and each codon specifies one amino acid. Mutations are changes in the DNA sequence that can be harmless, harmful, or occasionally beneficial. DNA replication is semi-conservative, meaning each new double helix keeps one original strand. Gregor Mendel discovered the basic laws of inheritance by breeding pea plants. Dominant alleles mask the effect of recessive alleles in heterozygous individuals. CRISPR-Cas9 lets scientists cut and edit DNA at precise locations, opening the door to gene therapy. The human genome contains roughly three billion base pairs and about twenty thousand protein-coding genes. Epigenetic marks such as DNA methylation switch genes on and off without changing the underlying sequence.

Mitosis divides one cell into two genetically identical daughter cells for growth and repair. Meiosis produces sperm and egg cells with half the usual number of chromosomes. Crossing over during meiosis shuffles genes between chromosomes and creates genetic variety. Cancer arises when mutations let cells ignore the signals that normally stop them from dividing. Stem cells can differentiate into many specialized cell types, which makes them valuable for regenerative medicine.

Evolution is the change in the inherited traits of populations over successive generations. Charles Darwin proposed natural selection as the main mechanism of evolution in On the Origin of Species. Individuals with traits better suited to their environment tend to survive and reproduce more. Genetic drift causes random changes in allele frequencies, especially in small populations. Speciation occurs when populations become reproductively isolated and diverge over time. Fossils record the history of life and show transitional forms such as Archaeopteryx. Homologous structures, like the bones in a human arm and a whale flipper, reveal common ancestry. Antibiotic resistance in bacteria is natural selection happening fast enough to watch. Convergent evolution produces similar features, like wings in bats and birds, in unrelated lineages. All known life shares a last universal common ancestor that lived more than three and a half billion years ago.

Ecology studies how organisms interact with each other and with their environment. Energy flows through ecosystems from producers to primary consumers to predators. Only about ten percent of the energy at one trophic level passes to the next. Decomposers such as fungi and bacteria recycle nutrients back into the soil. The carbon cycle moves carbon between the atmosphere, oceans, living things, and rocks. The nitrogen cycle depends on bacteria that fix atmospheric nitrogen into forms plants can use. Keystone species, like sea otters, have an outsized effect on the structure of their ecosystem. Biodiversity makes ecosystems more resilient to disease, drought, and disturbance. Habitat loss is the leading driver of species extinction worldwide. Symbiosis includes mutualism, where both partners benefit, and parasitism, where one benefits at the other's expense. Coral reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor yet support about a quarter of marine species. Rising ocean temperatures cause coral bleaching, as corals expel the algae that feed them.

Photosynthesis converts carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen using sunlight. The light-dependent reactions split water and produce ATP and NADPH in the thylakoid membranes. The Calvin cycle uses that ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into sugar. Cellular respiration breaks down glucose to release energy stored in its chemical bonds. Glycolysis happens in the cytoplasm and does not require oxygen. The Krebs cycle and the electron transport chain in mitochondria produce most of the ATP. Fermentation lets yeast and muscle cells make a little ATP when oxygen runs short. Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions by lowering their activation energy.

The human heart pumps blood through a closed circulatory system of arteries, veins, and capillaries. Red blood cells carry oxygen bound to hemoglobin from the lungs to every tissue. The lungs exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide across millions of tiny air sacs called alveoli. Neurons transmit electrical signals called action potentials along their axons. Synapses pass signals between neurons using chemical messengers called neurotransmitters. The brain contains about eighty-six billion neurons wired into vast networks. The immune system defends the body with white blood cells, antibodies, and inflammation. Vaccines train the immune system to recognize a pathogen without causing the disease. Hormones such as insulin travel through the bloodstream to regulate distant organs. The kidneys filter blood, remove waste, and balance the body's water and salt. The digestive system breaks food into molecules small enough to be absorbed in the small intestine. Trillions of gut bacteria make up the microbiome, which influences digestion, immunity, and even mood.
