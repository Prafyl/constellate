Mesopotamia, between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, is often called the cradle of civilization. The Sumerians invented cuneiform, one of the earliest writing systems, pressing wedge-shaped marks into clay tablets. Hammurabi of Babylon issued one of the first written law codes around 1750 BCE. Ancient Egypt flourished along the Nile, whose yearly floods made farming possible. The pharaohs built the pyramids of Giza as monumental tombs more than four thousand years ago. Hieroglyphs were finally deciphered after the discovery of the Rosetta Stone in 1799. The Indus Valley civilization built carefully planned cities like Mohenjo-daro with drains and standardized bricks. In China, the Shang dynasty left oracle bones that record the earliest Chinese writing. The Olmec of Mexico carved colossal stone heads and influenced later Mesoamerican cultures.

Classical Athens developed democracy, in which male citizens voted directly on laws. Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle laid the foundations of Western philosophy. Alexander the Great built an empire stretching from Greece to India before he died at thirty-two. The Roman Republic was governed by elected consuls and a powerful Senate. Julius Caesar crossed the Rubicon in 49 BCE, triggering a civil war that ended the Republic. Augustus became the first Roman emperor and began two centuries of relative peace called the Pax Romana. Roman engineers built roads, aqueducts, and concrete domes that still stand today. The Western Roman Empire fell in 476 CE, while the Eastern Empire continued as Byzantium for another thousand years. The Qin dynasty unified China and began the Great Wall, and the Han dynasty opened the Silk Road. The Maurya emperor Ashoka converted to Buddhism and spread its teachings across Asia.

The Islamic Golden Age made Baghdad a center of science, where scholars preserved Greek texts and advanced algebra. Medieval Europe was organized around feudalism, in which lords granted land to vassals in exchange for military service. The Crusades were a series of religious wars between European Christians and Muslim powers over the Holy Land. The Black Death killed roughly a third of Europe's population in the fourteenth century. The Mongol Empire under Genghis Khan became the largest contiguous land empire in history. The Magna Carta of 1215 limited the power of the English king and influenced later ideas of rights. The Mali Empire grew rich from gold and salt, and its ruler Mansa Musa became legendary for his wealth.

The Renaissance began in Italy and revived interest in classical art, science, and learning. Johannes Gutenberg's printing press, around 1440, made books cheaper and spread ideas faster than ever. Leonardo da Vinci and Michelangelo created masterpieces that defined Renaissance art. Martin Luther's Ninety-five Theses in 1517 sparked the Protestant Reformation. Christopher Columbus reached the Americas in 1492, beginning sustained contact between Europe and the New World. The Columbian Exchange moved crops, animals, and diseases between the hemispheres, reshaping diets and populations. The transatlantic slave trade forcibly transported about twelve million Africans to the Americas. The Scientific Revolution brought Copernicus, Galileo, and Newton, who changed how people understood the universe.

The American Revolution created the United States, whose Declaration of Independence proclaimed that all men are created equal. The French Revolution of 1789 overthrew the monarchy and spread ideals of liberty, equality, and fraternity. Napoleon Bonaparte rose from the chaos of the revolution to conquer much of Europe. The Haitian Revolution was the only successful slave revolt that founded an independent nation. The Industrial Revolution began in Britain with steam engines, textile mills, and coal. Factories drew millions of workers into crowded cities with long hours and dangerous conditions. Railways and telegraphs shrank distances and tied national economies together. European powers colonized most of Africa and much of Asia during the age of imperialism. Karl Marx argued that history is driven by class struggle between workers and owners.

World War I began in 1914 after the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo. Trench warfare on the Western Front killed millions of soldiers for tiny gains of territory. The Treaty of Versailles imposed harsh terms on Germany and planted seeds of resentment. The Russian Revolution of 1917 brought the Bolsheviks and Lenin to power. The Great Depression of the 1930s caused mass unemployment across the world. Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party exploited economic crisis and nationalism to seize power in Germany. World War II began in 1939 when Germany invaded Poland. The Holocaust was the systematic murder of six million Jews by the Nazi regime. The atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1945 ended the war in the Pacific. The United Nations was founded in 1945 to prevent future world wars.

The Cold War was a long rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union without direct war between them. The nuclear arms race gave both superpowers enough weapons to destroy civilization many times over. The Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962 brought the world to the edge of nuclear war. The Space Race ended with Apollo 11 landing astronauts on the Moon in 1969. India won independence from Britain in 1947, led by Mahatma Gandhi's movement of nonviolent resistance. Decolonization swept Africa and Asia as dozens of new nations gained independence. The American civil rights movement, led by figures like Martin Luther King Jr., fought racial segregation. The Berlin Wall fell in 1989, and the Soviet Union dissolved two years later. The internet and globalization connected economies and cultures at unprecedented speed.
